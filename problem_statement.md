Overnight hospital supervisors can’t tell which calls in a surge need a person first

When a fire, earthquake or outage hits at night, hospital phone lines fill with people trying to locate loved ones, offer help, or ask what the hospital is doing, and a few callers are in real danger. A small night crew has to answer them. Every call enters the same line and looks the same until a person picks up. Existing emergency plans tell hospitals to staff their phones around the clock with updated scripts, but not how to sort calls when staff are few. The usual fallbacks are to point callers to 911 or the ER, or to queue everyone for a human who is already busy. Phone triage is error-prone even for trained staff, and more so at night, and nothing sits between “a call comes in” and “a person handles it.”

Who lives with it

The overnight house supervisor, who decides alone who gets a human first.
Patients and families with urgent needs, stuck behind routine calls.
Switchboard staff and triage nurses, who receive transfers with no context about the caller.

Why now
Hospitals are already deploying AI voice agents for routine calls (Tampa General, per vendor Hyro), while a Nature Medicine study found a general-purpose AI tool under-triaged about half of true emergencies, so what automation should and shouldn’t do on a crisis line is an open question.

Why it matters

After the 2017 Las Vegas shooting, the family information line was overwhelmed and went down for two hours, and hospitals see an extreme jump in calls after sudden incidents (ASPR TRACIE, HHS).
In a study of recorded calls to Danish out-of-hours care, high-risk night calls were undertriaged about twice as often as day calls (13.2% vs 6.4%), at the hours when staffing is typically thinnest (BMJ Open).
